A study of four incremental games

How idle games work

Five games, taken apart. The machinery they share, the maths behind their pacing, and the design vocabulary that ties it together — followed by the original six-part essay series that named half of these ideas.

An idle game — also called an incremental or clicker game — looks trivial. A number goes up. You spend part of it to make the number go up faster. And yet a handful of these games have held millions of players for hundreds of hours, and their design rests on a small, surprisingly rich mathematical core. This guide pulls apart that core using four of the genre's most instructive titles, and then steps back to ask what all of it means for a game of our own.

We chose deliberately different games. Cookie Clicker is the clean baseline — almost pure exponential growth, one prestige layer, no way to lose. Exponential Idle pushes the maths to its absurd extreme, with numbers so large they need a custom type and four nested resets. Revolution Idle inverts the usual model: its income is a product raised to a power, so breadth matters more than depth. Leaf Blower Revolution is the busy, sprawling one, with a dozen currencies, hundreds of capped upgrades, and five resets stacked on top of each other. Together they cover most of the design space.

Part OneThe machinery of an idle game

Before the games, the concepts. These build on each other, so they are worth reading in order — the early ones are short and the later ones take more room.

The loop

Every game here runs on the same heartbeat. You have at least one currency — cookies, leaves, score — which arrives either by your own clicking or, more importantly, on its own. You spend that currency on assets: buildings, circles, tools, towers. Assets produce more currency, currency buys more assets, and the rate of production climbs. That is the entire genre. The craft is in the dials.

The first dial is the split between the two ways currency arrives. Active play — clicking the big cookie — is fast but bounded by your attention and your fingers. Passive play — the buildings that tick away in the background — is slow but free. A good idle game makes both matter: clicking gives an early edge and occasional bursts, but the buildings are what actually carry you forward. Cookie Clicker is almost too honest about this. Its Cursor building literally autoclicks once every ten seconds, so even a "clicking" asset is really just a slow generator.

The second dial is what a purchase gives you. Almost always it is a bit more production, and the interesting question — the one that occupies the rest of this chapter — is how much more, and how that amount changes as you buy more.

Purchase efficiency

When you can buy several things, which do you buy? The intuitive answer is to divide the cost by the gain and pick the smallest number: if a watering can costs 3 for +3 water and a pump costs 15 for +15, they are equally "efficient" and the choice is a wash. This is what one of the original essays called purchase efficiency, and it is the right first approximation.

It has a subtle flaw, though, and a commenter on that essay spotted it: a raw ratio ignores when the gain arrives. A +1 per second now is worth more than a +150 per second you can only afford an hour from now, because the early one compounds. The fix is to compare payback time — the cost divided by the rate of gain — and to remember that any buy made earlier also earns for the whole time you would have spent saving for the bigger one. In practice, good players of these games end up buying a small number of each thing and constantly re-scanning for the cheapest next gain. In Cookie Clicker, the sweet spot through most of the game is only about two to eight of each building before the next rung up becomes the better use of a cookie.

The design lesson is simple to state and easy to forget: if the game can rank purchases for the player — highlight the cheapest cost per unit of gain — it removes a layer of tedious arithmetic and lets the player focus on the interesting choice, which is usually what kind of gain to chase rather than which specific item.

Scaling: three families

Here is the actual equation under the whole genre, stated in Cookie Clicker's own terms:

price = base cost × 1.15 ^ (number already owned)

Each purchase makes the next one 15% dearer, and the production it adds is a flat step. The consequence is that the cost of the next purchase grows geometrically while the gain stays linear, so the time to afford the next one creeps upward — slowly at first, then relentlessly. That 15% is the single most-copied number in the genre, and it is worth knowing what it does: a multiplier of 1.15 doubles the price every 4.96 purchases and multiplies it by a thousand every 49.4. Those two numbers — five and fifty — are the whole rhythm of a Cookie-Clicker-style game. Roughly every five buys you feel the cost double; roughly every fifty, you feel it leap a thousandfold.

That is exponential scaling, the default "number go up" curve. The original essay series identified two others worth naming. The first is interdependent scaling, in which spending on one asset is what unlocks the next currency or tier entirely. The classic example is A Dark Room: you build houses before workers arrive, workers before you can gather enough to find a mine, and the mine opens an entirely new resource. Nothing "scales" smoothly here; the game advances through gates. The second family is derivative scaling, where you buy something that produces producers — colleges that produce mathematicians per second, while the mathematicians produce proofs. Production then compounds on top of itself, which the series called "exponentially exponential."

Derivative scaling is the most dangerous of the three, and the reason is instructive. If production grows without limit and nothing consumes it, the numbers race toward the point where the arithmetic itself breaks. The series' author put it bluntly: keep making rewards arrive faster and "your game is going to explode exponentially," until you hit the number cap and get NaN or Infinity. The antidote, he suggested, is a sink — a mechanism that eats the producers, so the growth is held in tension with decay.

Cost curves and cadence

The 1.15 curve is smooth to the point of being invisible. A commenter on the scaling essay made a lovely observation: you do not have to be smooth at all. You could approximate the same effect with plateaus and cliffs — five purchases that all cost 100, then five that cost 200, and so on, doubling every five. The average cost growth is similar, but the feel is completely different, and the commenter gave two reasons it might be better.

First, round numbers are easier to read. If prices are 100, 200, 500, 1000, the player sees at a glance how far away the next one is; if they are 132, 152, 174, 201, the precision is noise. Second — and this is the design insight — plateaus give a sense of getting ahead. While several purchases cost the same, buying one makes the next feel more affordable; then the cliff arrives and marks a new obstacle to overcome. The flat stretch is relief, the cliff is drama. A smooth curve gives you neither.

So the cost curve is not just a difficulty setting; it is a pacing instrument. Exponential, stepped, or some hybrid — each produces a different emotional rhythm, and the choice is as much about feel as about balance.

Discounts

Sooner or later a game offers to make things cheaper, and here there is a trap. Discounts stack multiplicatively, which means equal headline steps are not equal. The gap between a 1% discount and a 2% discount is nothing; the gap between 98% and 99% is enormous, because that last percent halves every remaining cost. A designer who scales discounts linearly will find that the late-game discounts are wildly more powerful than the early ones, distorting the whole economy.

The rule that follows is easy to remember and important: give discounts a larger headline effect at low levels and a smaller one at high levels, and never let the total reach 100%. Cookie Clicker follows this to the letter — its many discounts are multiplicative and none of them reach zero, and the maximum achievable is about a 35% reduction. The one legitimate use of a 100% discount is as a deliberate phase-change: not a stat you accumulate, but an event that says "for the next while, buying is free," which is a very different thing.

Idle time and offline

Idling is the genre's defining feature, and it comes with a strict rule from the original series: idle intervals should be consistent or grow, never shrink. If the game demands your attention more and more often, it has stopped being something you can leave running while you work, which was the entire point. The author suggested thinking in terms of a play-to-idle ratio — one minute of play for fifteen of idle, say — and warned that idling must not become a "fancy timer with no real purpose," meaning it has to actually matter to progress.

What happens while the game is closed is a separate design decision, and the four games solve it four different ways. Cookie Clicker uses a capped floor: offline you earn 5% of your normal rate for the first hour, dropping to 0.5% after, and it is switched off by default until you buy the relevant upgrade. Exponential Idle instead replays the simulation while you were away, stepping the same tick function many times to catch up, with no hard day limit. Revolution Idle treats offline time as a literal resource: time away accumulates a currency that buys simulation speed, so being away does not accrue value so much as accelerate it later. Leaf Blower Revolution takes a quarter of your best-ever rate and replays every subsystem, capped at a day (extendable to a week).

There is no single correct answer, but the shared shape is: choose an offline model, cap it, and make the return feel like a reward — usually with a summary of what you gathered.

Prestige and reset layers

The original series never covered prestige — the voluntary reset that trades your progress for a permanent multiplier — which is a striking omission, because it is arguably the defining structure of the modern genre. Our research extended the vocabulary to fill that gap, because prestige is where the long game lives.

The basic idea: at some point it becomes faster to throw everything away and start again with a permanent bonus than to grind onward. Cookie Clicker does this once: when you ascend, you trade your cookies for heavenly chips, where the number of chips is the cube root of your lifetime cookies divided by a trillion, and each chip gives a flat +1% to production forever. That cube root matters. It means a million-fold increase in cookies only gives a hundred-fold increase in chips, so a reset is generous but sharply diminishing — the game is telling you, gently, when it is time to stop and restart.

The other three games turn this single reset into a stack. Exponential Idle has four nested layers, where resetting the top one wipes the ones below but grants a bigger, slower reward. Revolution Idle has five. Leaf Blower Revolution has four, and here is the cleanest idea in the whole genre: each layer rewards a different metric. One asks how much you collected, one asks how big your multipliers got, one asks how far up a tower you climbed, one asks how many mechanics you engaged with. No single action covers them all, so no single strategy dominates.

If you are designing a game, the question is not "should there be prestige" but "what should each reset ask of the player, and what does it give back?" A reset that asks the same question twice is just a longer grind; a reset that asks a new question is a new game.

Active play: clicks, streaks, events

Clicks need a job. If clicking is unbounded, a script does it better than a human and the interface becomes a formality. If clicking does nothing, the game is a screensaver. The essays and the games offer a spread of solutions.

Cookie Clicker's answer is the variable-ratio reward: golden cookies appear at random, on a schedule that runs from five to fifteen minutes, and give big temporary buffs. This is the same psychology as a slot machine, and the author of the reward-frequency essay said so explicitly — the game taps a reward pattern the player cannot fully predict, which is exactly what keeps them glancing at the screen. Exponential Idle uses a bounded burst: a button that accelerates time, but caps itself at about three times normal speed, so it is a boost rather than a shortcut. Leaf Blower Revolution uses a decaying streak: a combo multiplier that climbs as you keep collecting leaves and decays within seconds if you stop, rewarding sustained attention. And Revolution Idle, tellingly, has no click lane at all — its "active" layer is the scripting system you use to automate the game.

The through-line: active play should pay better right now than idling, without ever making idle progress pointless. That balance — a reward for showing up that does not punish you for leaving — is the whole trick.

Caps, walls, and failure

Nothing grows forever, and how a game handles its limits says a lot about it. There are two kinds of wall. A soft wall just gets more expensive: Cookie Clicker's 1.15 ladder is one long soft wall, with occasional hard steps where a new building costs ten times the last. A hard wall is a ceiling — a building that can only go to level 30, a shop item you can buy three of, an "Infinity" beyond which production simply stops. Leaf Blower Revolution is the hard-wall game: 898 of its 1022 shop upgrades have a maximum count, so plateaus are the norm and only a few uncapped value multipliers keep climbing.

The most interesting mechanic in this whole area is a hard wall that moves, but only when you reset. Exponential Idle caps production at a point called Infinity; no amount of normal play gets past it. Only a higher-tier prestige raises the cap, from one ceiling to the next, through a fixed ladder of ever-larger exponents. This is brilliant because it converts the vaguest of goals — "go faster" — into a concrete, deliberate decision: the only way through is to stop and bank a reset. Revolution Idle does something similar with magnitude thresholds that double as both resets and walls.

And then there is the thing all four games lack: a way to lose. None of them can be failed. This is the genre's biggest unexamined assumption, and it is the exact assumption our own game — a tower defence where upgrading the enemy stream too fast can kill you — was built to break. When there is no losing, walls can only ever be delays. When there is losing, a wall becomes a genuine dilemma, and we will come back to that in the last part.

Two-sided scaling (when there are enemies)

Every game in the main study is one-sided. You buy production, production earns money, and the only thing pushing back is the cost curve. Even Revolution Idle, which looks like a battle, has no enemy — its "circles" are just generators. This is a real blind spot, because our tower defence is two-sided: the thing you invest in also gets stronger and tries to kill you. So we added a fifth game, NGU Idle, precisely because it has an enemy side.

NGU Idle's combat is refreshingly simple, and scale-free. Your health is ten times your Attack, and you regenerate your Defense divided by twenty each second. When you fight a boss, the damage you deal per second is your Attack minus the boss's Defense; the damage you take is the boss's Attack minus your Defense. So the whole fight reduces to a race between two pairs of numbers, and the strategy is a ratio: once your Defense exceeds five times the boss's Attack, you take nothing and can safely skip ahead. Because your health is tied to your Attack the same way the enemy's health is tied to its Attack, the two sides are measured on one ruler — which is exactly the property that makes the danger legible to a player.

The interesting part is how the enemy side grows. Each boss from the fifth to the twentieth is five times the previous one; from the twenty-first onward, ten times. Meanwhile the reward for resetting — a permanent multiplier proportional to the bosses you have beaten — grows by only about two times per boss, and less as you raise the difficulty. The enemy ladder is therefore steeper than the reward ladder, deliberately. Raw prestige can never keep pace, which forces the player to lean on the game's other systems to stay ahead. That is the same mechanism our game needs: if upgrading the stream were self-sufficiently rewarding, there would be no risk. The gap between the two ladders is what creates the decision.

NGU also adds two forms of soft enrage, both worth stealing. Titans gain one percent more power with every attack they take, so a slow kill becomes a losing one; and a thousand-floor endless dungeon increases each floor's enemies by five percent, an endless gentle ramp. Neither is a hard wall, but both convert "I am barely winning" into "I am on a timer." And then the twist that makes NGU a cautionary tale as much as a model: it has no lose condition. Being defeated teleports you to a safe zone with no penalty at all. That safety net is what lets its enemy ladder be as brutal as ten-times-per-step without feeling unfair. Our game removes the net, and the final part is about what changes when it is gone.

Big numbers

Idle games get to numbers that ordinary arithmetic cannot hold. A 64-bit floating-point number, the normal kind, tops out around 1.8 × 10308; past that it becomes Infinity and the game breaks. Most web incrementals solve this with a library called break_infinity.js, which extends the range to roughly ee16 — a one followed by 1016 zeros. That is plenty for most games.

Exponential Idle needed more, so it rolls its own type: every number is stored as a triple of sign, exponent, and depth, where depth is how many layers of "e" you are stacked under. This is what lets the game casually print numbers with thousands of e's. The lesson for a designer is unglamorous but unavoidable: decide how large your numbers can get, and how you will represent them, before you design an endgame that depends on them.

A shared vocabulary

A few terms are worth fixing before we go further, because they recur constantly. A resource is anything you can spend; an asset is anything that produces. A resource can be an asset — cookies earn cookies — but most assets are not directly spent. An upgrade is anything that changes the rules: a multiplier, a new unlock, an automation, a discount. The original series listed a dozen distinct classes of upgrade (auto-acquisition rate, harvest interval, decay rate, global speed, and so on), which is useful mostly as a checklist: if you can classify every upgrade in your game against that list, you are far less likely to accidentally build the same one three times.

Holding all of this together is one idea the series never named but every game obeys: the shape of a game — the order in which its systems unlock, and how long each phase lasts. Cookie Clicker's shape, for instance, is "dozens of hours per run, with the endgame cliffs taking multiple days each." The shape is the game's promise about how much time it is asking for, and it is decided mostly by the numbers on this part's pages.

Part ThreeFour games, four philosophies

With the vocabulary in hand, here is how each game actually spends it. The four were chosen to disagree with each other as much as possible.

Cookie Clicker — the clean baseline

An endless sum-of-generators on one 1.15 ladder, punctuated by ten-fold buildings, tier doublings and golden-cookie bursts — safe by construction, with no way to lose.

Cookie Clicker is the genre's Rosetta stone. Its economy is a single ladder: twenty buildings, each costing 1.15 times the previous unit of its kind, each producing a flat amount. The buildings themselves are laid out along a steep staircase — a Farm costs about eleven times a Grandma, a Mine about eleven times a Farm, and the endgame buildings leap by factors of a hundred and sixty or more — so the game is really a sequence of soft walls, each followed by a fresh, powerful generator. It is the multiplicative version of the plateau-and-cliff idea from the scaling essay, except the plateaus are within a building and the cliffs are between them.

Its upgrade system layers on top of that baseline. Each building has fifteen tiered upgrades that double its production, plus "kitten" upgrades driven by milk (which comes from achievements, tying your collection to your economy), plus synergies in which one building boosts another, plus a research tree, plus a heavenly tree that unlocks only after your first reset. The design is generous to the point of excess, and it works because the base ladder is so legible that players can absorb all the extras without losing the thread.

Prestige happens once. You spend your cookies on heavenly chips, the cube root of your lifetime total, each worth a flat +1% forever. The active layer is golden cookies — random, buff-granting, on a five-to-fifteen-minute schedule. Offline income is a small, capped fraction of your normal rate. And there is no lose condition at all; the only stakes are time.

Exponential Idle — the math temple

A double-exponential temple you periodically collapse: four nested resets, each reward read off a cumulative product, with a hard wall that only a higher layer can move.

Exponential Idle is what happens when a mathematician designs a clicker. Its central object is not a list of buildings but an equation: a value f(t) that grows by a factor of e(b · x · φ · τ · dt) every tick, where the letters are multipliers you earn from different systems. You do not buy generators in the ordinary sense; you buy variables in a change-of-variables tower, each one fed by the last.

Its costs are the most aggressive in the genre. A regular generator's price follows what we can only call a double exponential — the multiplier on each purchase grows with the price itself, so a buy might cost six times the last early on and three hundred times the last once the numbers are large. This is derivative scaling taken to its logical end, and it is why the game needs its custom big-number type.

Prestige is not one reset but four, nested. Reset the bottom layer and you keep the one above; reset a higher layer and it wipes everything below, granting a much larger, much slower reward. Each layer's payout is read from a cumulative product — a running total that includes every reset you have ever done — so even though the rewards look flat or diminishing in the moment, they compound across the whole history of the game. And the whole thing is capped by Infinity, a hard wall that no ordinary play can pass; only a higher-tier reset raises it, one ceiling at a time, through exponents that grow from a hundred to a million. It is the most elegant "no free progress" rule we found.

Revolution Idle — breadth through exponents

Breadth is mandatory: income is a product raised to a power, so walls are level caps and magnitude thresholds, and the active layer is automation scripting.

Revolution Idle starts with ten circles, each a colour, each producing score as it completes laps. You upgrade a circle's level to make it faster, and you ascend it — resetting it to level five — once it hits a cap. That is the early game, and it is almost gentle: the cost multiplier on each circle is a modest 1.20 to 1.56, far flatter than Cookie Clicker's ladder.

The gentleness is a trap, because the shape of the economy is different. Your income is not the sum of the circles; it is the product of all their multipliers, raised to a common exponent. That single change rewrites the strategy. In a product economy, breadth is mandatory: ten circles at multiplier m are worth m to the tenth power, so neglecting any one of them is catastrophic. And because the whole product is then raised to an exponent, every upgrade lands as a cliff rather than a slope. Walls, accordingly, are not prices but counts: a hard level cap of one hundred plus ten per ascension.

Prestige is a stack of five, each gated by a magnitude threshold — ten billion score for the first, then thresholds that climb into the numbers with hundreds of digits. Promotion then layers four boosts on top, each with its own experience curve, which the community has mapped to the exact formula. And the game has no clicking at all: its "active" layer is a macro system in which you script the game's own automation, with the wonderful wrinkle that automation degrades at very high speed, so raw speed is a risk you have to manage rather than a pure win.

Leaf Blower Revolution — many currencies, capped cliffs

A dozen parallel leaf currencies and capped cliffs (898 of 1022 upgrades are finite); four resets that each ask a different question.

Leaf Blower Revolution is the maximalist of the four, and the most instructive about what happens when a game keeps bolting systems onto a simple start. You begin by blowing leaves off a screen. Basic leaves buy better tools; better leaves unlock new areas; each area has its own shop, its own leaf type, and its own chain of upgrades. Before long you are juggling dozens of currencies, and the game's complexity is its actual content.

Its cost curves are geometric but, crucially, capped. Each upgrade follows cost = base × cost_exp^level, where the growth factor ranges from about 1.5 (doubling every 1.7 levels) up to 4 (doubling every half-level) — far steeper per purchase than Cookie Clicker. But most of these upgrades have a maximum count, so they plateau and stay plateaued. Only a handful of uncapped value multipliers keep climbing. The result is a game of buying out finite utilities and then rotating to the next system — the plateau is the default state, and progress comes in bursts when a new lane opens.

Its four resets are the clever part. Each one rewards a different metric: how much you collected, how big your multipliers grew, how high you climbed a tower, how many mechanics you engaged with. Because they ask different questions, they cannot be satisfied by the same action, and the game never reduces to one optimal loop. The active layer is a decaying combo multiplier that climbs as you keep collecting and collapses within seconds if you stop — the cleanest active-play reward of the four. Offline income is a quarter of your best-ever rate, capped at a day.

NGU Idle — the two-sided race

The only game here with an enemy: a scale-free stats race where the enemy ladder outpaces the reward ladder on purpose — and where losing still costs nothing.

NGU Idle is an enormous, finished, cult-classic incremental from 2019, and the reason we studied it is narrow: it is the one reference game with a genuine enemy side. Its economy is otherwise classic — training energy and magic to raise your core Stats, augmenting them, and resetting — but threaded through all of it is a combat system that runs on two matching stat pairs.

Fighting reduces to arithmetic. Your Health is 10 × Attack and you regenerate Defense / 20 per second; the damage each side deals is its attack minus the other's defense. Bosses from the fifth to the twentieth are each five times the previous, and from the twenty-first each ten times — a ladder that outpaces the player's prestige reward, which grows only about two times per boss and less at higher difficulties. Titans add one percent of power with every hit they take, and the thousand-floor ITOPOD dungeon scales five percent per floor. Every one of these is a pressure that punishes standing still.

What makes it a reference rather than a template is the safety net. Losing a fight in NGU Idle sends you to a safe zone with no penalty; there is no way to actually fail. That is what licenses the brutal ten-times-per-step enemy growth. Our game keeps the two-sided structure and throws away the net, which is the whole design question — and Part Five takes it up directly.

The mismatch is the machinery. Part FiveInto our own game

Our game inverts the genre's central assurance. It is a clicker tower defence: an enemy stream walks in from the right toward guns on the left; dead enemies become currency; that currency buys towers and — critically — stronger enemies. Upgrading the stream raises your income and the threat at the same time, and if you push the latter too far, you lose. Everything above was read with that inversion in mind, and it points at five things worth building deliberately.

One ladder, two sides. Use a single shared cost curve for both towers and the enemy stream — a 1.15-style ladder is the obvious starting point, because it is the one pattern every player already understands. When the "next unit costs a bit more" rhythm applies to the thing trying to kill you and the thing defending you, the trade-off becomes instantly legible.

Make the stream the tempting buy. From Cookie Clicker we learned that players follow purchase efficiency almost reflexively — they will buy whatever gives the most gain per unit spent. If upgrading the stream is the efficient buy (which it should be, since it is the risk), the player will naturally reach for it. But pair that temptation with a preview: show the projected enemy health and arrival rate next to the current tower damage, so that "I can afford to push this" and "I will survive if I do" are two separate, visible facts. NGU Idle hands us a ready-made instrument for this: because both sides are measured on one scale (health is ten times attack, for everyone), a single ratio — your defence divided by the wave's attack — states exactly how safe you are. Surface that ratio before the player commits, and the goal is met: losing feels like a decision, never a trap.

Let the wall move only when you bank a reset. Exponential Idle's moving Infinity wall is the best idea we found for a risk game. Make the cap on total threat something that advances only when the player completes a reset, never for free during a run. That single rule turns the dangerous "accelerate the stream" action into a deliberate, between-run decision, and makes each reset a genuine pivot rather than a chore.

Bark the threat super-linearly. For the risk to be real, enemy pressure has to rise faster than your income can. If income scales roughly linearly with stream upgrades while enemy health and speed scale super-linearly — the way Revolution Idle's product-then-exponent economy makes every upgrade a cliff — then the stream lever stays attractive right up to the point where it becomes fatal. That is exactly the tension we want, and it is a property of the curve shapes, not of any single number.

Build the horde as a rhythm, and reward showing up. Structure the waves as ten-fold health walls followed by farms, one boss per era, so the difficulty reads as movement rather than a monotone ramp. On top of it, put a fast-decaying kill-streak multiplier — Leaf Blower Revolution's combo, which we judged the cleanest active reward of the four — and schedule elite or treasure enemies on a five-to-fifteen-minute escalating clock, borrowing the golden cookie's variable-ratio pull. Active play should pay better now, without making idling pointless. Offline should be a capped quarter-rate with a welcome-back summary, so leaving is rewarded but never trivialises the risk.

None of the four games can be lost. That is the space we are stepping into, and the research suggests the way to do it well is not to add a fail state on top of a safe incremental — it is to make the act of growing be the danger. The stream is the economy; the economy is the threat; the player holds both levers at once.
